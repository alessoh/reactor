# Getting real reviews — Design Your Own Nuclear Reactor

## The rule that matters, and the exception that helps you

Amazon's Community Guidelines forbid compensating anyone for a review, and free or discounted
product normally counts as compensation. **Books are the documented exception.** Amazon's own
policy states that authors and publishers may provide free or discounted copies, provided you
do not require a review in exchange and do not attempt to influence what it says.

So the legitimate play is: give the book away freely to people who would genuinely want it,
ask for an honest opinion, and accept whatever comes back. Three things keep you safe:

- **Never require a review.** Ask; do not condition the copy on it.
- **Never suggest a rating or supply wording.** The moment you send draft text, it becomes a
  fabricated review.
- **Ask reviewers to disclose** that they received a free copy. The FTC requires disclosure of
  a material connection, and it costs you nothing in credibility with this audience.

A technical readership is unusually good at smelling manufactured praise. Twelve specific,
mixed reviews from real engineers will sell more copies than fifty generic five-star ones —
and the generic ones are what get a listing flagged.

## Who to actually approach

This book has three natural constituencies. Aim for roughly 25–40 outreach emails to land
10–15 reviews; a 30–40 per cent response rate is normal when the targeting is good.

**1. Nuclear engineering faculty and lab instructors.** Departments at Penn State, Michigan,
Texas A&M, Illinois Urbana-Champaign, NC State, Purdue, Wisconsin, Georgia Tech, Oregon
State, Missouri S&T, and MIT all run undergraduate reactor-theory sequences. The person you
want is whoever teaches the sophomore or junior reactor physics course — usually listed on the
department's course pages. Your pitch to them is not "please review my book," it is "here is a
free simulator for your course, and the manual that goes with it."

**2. American Nuclear Society student chapters.** Chapter officers are listed publicly and are
perpetually looking for activities. A free browser tool that lets members design and compare
reactors is a ready-made chapter meeting.

**3. Science communicators and technical reviewers.** Nuclear-focused YouTube channels,
Substack writers covering energy policy, and the moderators of r/nuclear and r/NuclearPower.
Reddit will not tolerate a promotional post, but a genuine "I rebuilt my 1993 reactor design
program, here it is free" post with the story behind it is exactly the kind of thing that
subreddit upvotes. Lead with the tool, not the book.

**Also worth a note:** *Nuclear News* (ANS), *Nuclear Engineering International*, and *The
Physics Teacher* (AAPT) all run book review sections. Those are editorial reviews, not Amazon
reviews, but a line from any of them can go in your Author Central Editorial Reviews section,
which appears on the listing above the customer reviews.

## Editorial Reviews — the legitimate place for praise on your listing

Author Central has an **Editorial Reviews** section that displays above customer reviews on
your product page. It is meant for attributed quotes from named, real sources: a professor, a
practising engineer, a trade publication, another author.

This is where an endorsement you solicited belongs. Standard publishing practice is that you
may ask someone for a blurb, and they may ask you for a starting draft — that is normal and
above board, because a named person puts their name to it and can edit or refuse it. What is
never acceptable is placing that text in the customer review section as though a stranger
wrote it.

Fill these slots as endorsements arrive:

| Section | What to put there |
| --- | --- |
| Review | Two or three attributed quotes, name and affiliation |
| From the Author | Two paragraphs on why you rebuilt a 1993 program |
| From the Back Cover | The back cover copy from `kdp-listing.md` |
| About the Author | Your bio and prior titles |

## Amazon Vine

If you publish the paperback through KDP and own the ISBN, you may be eligible to enrol in
**Vine for KDP**. Amazon supplies copies to its own vetted reviewers, and the reviews carry a
"Vine Customer Review of Free Product" badge. There is a fee, enrolment requires fewer than 30
existing reviews, and you cannot choose the reviewers or influence what they say.

It is the only sanctioned way to buy access to reviewers rather than reviews. For a technical
niche title it tends to produce a small number of thoughtful reviews, which is exactly the
profile you want.

## A realistic timeline

| Week | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Before launch | Build the outreach list. 30–40 named people, not addresses scraped in bulk. |
| Launch week | Send templates 1–3. Post the tool, not the book, to r/nuclear. |
| Weeks 2–4 | Follow up once with non-responders. Once only. |
| Week 4 | Enrol in Vine if you have fewer than 30 reviews. |
| Ongoing | The back-of-book page works quietly forever. It is the highest-yield item here. |

Ten to fifteen genuine reviews in the first quarter is a good outcome for a technical title,
and it is enough to make the listing look alive. Chasing a higher number faster is precisely
what gets accounts flagged.
